What Is a Prop Bet? Player Props vs. Game Props Explained

By Scott Olmer, designated author and editorial owner. Published November 27, 2026.

Produced through an automated AI workflow. How our editorial process works.

A prop bet (short for “proposition bet”) is a bet on a specific thing happening in a game, not on the final result. Instead of picking who wins, you’re picking something like whether a player scores more than a set number of points, or whether a game has a score in the first five minutes.

That’s the whole prop bet meaning in one line. The rest of this guide covers the main types of props, how they grade, where platform rules matter, how sportsbook props differ from DFS pick’em entries, and how to research a prop before you decide anything.

What are prop bets? The prop bet definition in plain English

Most traditional bets are about the outcome of the game:

  • Moneyline: which team wins. (New to this one? See what is a moneyline bet.)
  • Spread: whether a team wins or loses by more than a set margin.
  • Total: whether both teams combined score over or under a set number.

A prop bet sets the final result aside and focuses on one smaller event inside the game. The prop bet definition is simply: a wager on a specific occurrence or stat, where the final score does not decide whether you win.

Because of that, a prop can cash even if the team you like loses, and a prop can lose even if the team you like wins by 30. Props are about the detail, not the scoreboard.

What is a prop bet in sports? The three main types

Props come in a few flavors. The examples below use made-up players and teams. They are fictional, for teaching only, and are not real lines or real stats.

Player props

A player prop is about one player’s individual stats. This is the most popular kind of prop by far.

Fictional examples:

  • Player A over 24.5 points
  • Player B under 6.5 rebounds
  • Player C over 1.5 made threes
  • Player D over 34.5 PRA (points + rebounds + assists combined)

Player props can be single stats (points, rebounds, passing yards, shots on goal) or combo stats. If you’ve seen “PRA” on a prop board, our guide on what PRA means explains it. Some player props are yes/no instead of a number, like “Will Player E record a double-double?” (see what is a double-double).

Game props

Game props are about the game itself, or a team, rather than one player.

Fictional examples:

  • Will the first score of Team X vs. Team Y be a touchdown? (Yes / No)
  • Total first-half points over 44.5
  • Will there be overtime? (Yes / No)
  • Which team scores first?

Game props are different from a standard moneyline, spread or total because they zoom in on a specific event or slice of the game (first score, first half, a single quarter) rather than the final result.

Futures-style props

Some props are about something that won’t be settled for weeks or months. These are often called futures or season-long props.

Fictional examples:

  • Player F over 1,100.5 season receiving yards
  • Team Z over 9.5 regular-season wins
  • Will Player G win the league’s top individual award? (Yes / No)

These take longer to grade, and injuries or roster changes along the way can affect them a lot.

How prop bets grade: Over/Under and Yes/No

Most props come in one of two formats.

Over/Under (number) props. The platform posts a line, like 24.5 points, and you pick over or under.

  • Fictional Player A over 24.5 points: if Player A finishes with 25 or more, the over wins. With 24 or fewer, the under wins.
  • The “.5” is there on purpose. A player can’t score half a point, so a line ending in .5 can’t land exactly on the number.

Yes/No props. You pick whether something happens.

  • “Will Player E record a double-double?” grades Yes if it happens and No if it doesn’t.

Pushes, voids and DNP: why platform rules matter

This is where beginners get surprised. The rules for edge cases depend on the platform you use, so always read its house rules.

  • Push: If a line is a whole number (say, 25 points) and the player finishes with exactly 25, many platforms treat that as a push, which usually means the stake is returned. Some handle it differently.
  • Void: A prop can be voided or cancelled, for example if a game is postponed or a line was posted in error. What happens to a voided leg (especially inside a multi-pick entry) varies by platform.
  • DNP (did not play): If a player doesn’t play, many platforms void that player’s props. Others have different rules, such as requiring the player to take the field or play a minimum amount for the prop to stand.
  • Overtime: Many platforms count overtime stats toward player props, but not all, and some game props are specific to regulation.

None of this is universal. Check the rules on the platform you use before you assume how a prop will grade.

Sportsbook props vs. DFS pick’em entries

You’ll see player props in two main places, and they work differently.

Sportsbook props. A sportsbook posts a line and attaches odds to each side (for example, -115 on the over and -105 on the under). Those odds determine what a winning bet pays. Each prop can be its own standalone bet, or combined into a parlay.

DFS pick’em entries (for example, PrizePicks and Underdog). These are daily fantasy sports contests, not traditional bets. You pick more or less (or higher or lower) on several player stat projections and combine them into one entry. Individual picks generally don’t show odds. Instead, the payout is set by the entry: how many picks you make, the entry type, and the platform’s payout table. Availability and rules vary by state and platform.

Neither format is “better.” They’re different products with different rules and payout structures. If you use one of these platforms, see does Propeller support PrizePicks? and does Propeller support Underdog?

How to research a prop bet

Research won’t make an outcome certain, but it can help you understand what a line is asking for. A simple, general checklist:

  1. Minutes or snaps. Stats need playing time. A change in expected minutes can matter more than any recent hot streak.
  2. Role. Is the player starting or coming off the bench? Did a teammate’s absence change who handles the ball or gets targets?
  3. Recent usage vs. a longer sample. Look at recent games, but compare them with a longer stretch. A couple of big games can make a trend look stronger than it is.
  4. Matchup. Pace, opponent style, and how the opponent defends a position can all shift a stat line up or down.
  5. The line itself. Ask what the number implies. Over 24.5 points is a question about whether the player gets to 25, not whether they “play well.”

Prop bet FAQ

What is a prop bet?

A prop bet is a bet on a specific thing happening in a game, such as a player’s points total or which team scores first, rather than on the final result.

What does “prop” stand for?

It’s short for “proposition.” A prop bet is a proposition about a specific event or stat.

What’s the difference between player props and game props?

Player props are about one player’s individual stats. Game props are about the game or a team, like the first score, a half’s total, or whether there’s overtime.

What happens to a player prop if the player doesn’t play?

It depends on the platform. Many void the prop if the player doesn’t play, but rules differ, so check the platform’s house rules.

Are PrizePicks and Underdog prop bets?

They’re daily fantasy pick’em contests that use player stat projections. Individual picks don’t carry odds; the payout is set by the entry type and number of picks. Rules and availability vary by state.

Is a Propeller score the chance a prop wins?

No. A Propeller score reflects research signal strength, not win odds or a win probability.
