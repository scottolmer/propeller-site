What Is a Double-Double in Basketball? The 10-and-10 Rule Explained

By Scott Olmer, designated author and editorial owner. Published November 20, 2026.

Produced through an automated AI workflow. How our editorial process works.

A double-double in basketball is when a player reaches 10 or more in two of the five main counting stats (points, rebounds, assists, steals, and blocks) in a single game. The most common version is “10 and 10” (10+ points and 10+ rebounds), but any two of the five categories count.

If you have seen “double double basketball” on a stat sheet or a player prop board and wondered what it means, this guide covers the rule, a few fictional examples, how a yes/no double-double prop typically grades, and what kinds of players tend to get there.

Double-double meaning: the rule in plain English

Here is the whole rule:

  • Pick any two of these five stats: points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks.
  • The player needs 10 or more in each of those two stats.
  • It has to happen in one game. Stats do not carry over between games.

That’s it. Exactly 10 counts. 9 does not. There is no “close enough.”

A few things that do not count toward a double-double:

  • Turnovers, fouls, minutes, or made threes. They’re tracked, but they aren’t among the five categories.
  • Combined totals. 6 offensive rebounds plus 5 defensive rebounds is 11 rebounds, which counts as one rebounding category, not two. And 20 points does not count as “two tens.”
  • Combo stats like PRA. Points + Rebounds + Assists is a single added-up number. A player can post 30 PRA without a double-double. (More on PRA in our guide: What does PRA mean?)

What about a triple-double?

A triple-double is the same idea with three categories: 10 or more in three of the five stats in one game. Every triple-double is also a double-double, because the player cleared 10 in at least two categories along the way. It’s much rarer. We’ll cover it in its own guide; for now, just know it’s the “three tens” version.

What is a double-double? Three fictional stat lines

Fictional example 1: a double-double
Player A: 18 points, 11 rebounds, 3 assists, 1 steal, 2 blocks.
Points (18) and rebounds (11) are both 10 or more. That’s a double-double.

Fictional example 2: a near miss
Player B: 10 points, 9 rebounds, 4 assists, 0 steals, 1 block.
Points hit 10, but rebounds stopped at 9. Only one category reached 10, so this is not a double-double. Close doesn’t count.

Fictional example 3: a triple-double
Player C: 14 points, 10 rebounds, 11 assists, 2 steals, 0 blocks.
Points, rebounds, and assists are all 10 or more. That’s a triple-double, and it also satisfies the double-double rule.

Quick check: count how many of the five categories are at 10 or more. Two or more = double-double. Three or more = triple-double. One or zero = neither.

How a double-double prop typically grades

Some player prop platforms offer a simple yes/no market: “Will this player record a double-double?” The way it typically works:

  • Yes wins if the player finishes the game with 10+ in at least two of the five categories.
  • No wins if they don’t.
  • A triple-double usually counts as “yes,” since it includes a double-double.
  • It’s graded on the official final box score.

The details can differ from one platform to another, so check your platform’s rules before you decide anything. In particular, look at:

  • Overtime. Many platforms count overtime stats toward player props, but not all treat every market the same way.
  • DNP and voids. If a player doesn’t play, or leaves very early, the prop may be voided, refunded, or handled another way depending on the platform.
  • Stat corrections. Official stats are sometimes adjusted after a game. Platforms have their own policy on whether late corrections change a result.

Don’t assume. The platform’s own rules page is the final word.

Which players tend to get double-doubles?

In general terms, double-doubles follow a player’s role:

  • Bigs (centers and power forwards) most often get there through points + rebounds. They play near the basket, where many rebounds land, and they often score close to the rim.
  • Guards, especially lead ball-handlers, more often get there through points + assists, because the offense runs through them.
  • Rebounding wings and forwards sometimes reach it through points + rebounds.
  • Steals and blocks are the rarest path. Reaching 10 of either in one game is very uncommon, so they almost never drive a double-double, though they do count.

Other things that tend to matter: minutes (more time on the floor means more chances), usage (how often the offense runs through the player), and pace (faster games create more possessions, shots, and missed shots to rebound). Even players who reach double-doubles often can fall short on a given night. Foul trouble, a blowout that shortens minutes, or a cold shooting game can all leave someone at 9.

Double-double FAQ

What is a double-double in basketball?

It’s when a player gets 10 or more in two of the five counting stats (points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks) in one game.

Do steals and blocks count toward a double-double?

Yes. Any two of the five categories count, including steals and blocks. They’re just much harder to reach 10 in.

Is 10 points and 9 rebounds a double-double?

No. Both categories need to be 10 or more. 10 points and 9 rebounds is a near miss.

Is a triple-double also a double-double?

Yes. A triple-double means 10+ in three categories, which includes at least two. Most yes/no double-double props treat it as “yes,” but check your platform’s rules.

Does overtime count for a double-double prop?

It depends on the platform. Many include overtime stats, but check the rules on the platform you use.

What’s the difference between a double-double and PRA?

A double-double needs 10+ in two separate categories. PRA adds points, rebounds, and assists into one total. They measure different things.
