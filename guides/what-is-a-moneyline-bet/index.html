What is a moneyline bet? Read the winner, price and payout

By Scott Olmer, designated author and editorial owner. Draft produced October 16, 2026.

Produced through an automated AI workflow. How our editorial process works.

A moneyline bet is a wager on which team or player wins the named game, match or period. The price tells you the potential profit relative to your stake; it does not add or subtract points. Check whether the market has two or three outcomes and which game period it covers.

This guide explains the terms using fictional prices. Propeller researches player props and does not accept or place wagers.

Winner first, price second

Read a moneyline as two separate fields: the selection says who you are choosing to win; the price sets the potential profit if that selection wins.

For a fictional “Team A to win” selection priced at -150, the minus number is not a demand that Team A win by 150 points. A win by one point or ten points satisfies that winner selection, subject to the stated market rules. A point spread changes the result being compared; a moneyline price does not.

American prices also appear on other types of markets. A minus sign alone cannot tell you that you are looking at a moneyline. Start with the market label.

What does a fictional $10 return?

Fictional two-team example. Not current odds or a recommendation. Team A -150 and Team B +130 are separate alternatives in the same illustrative full-game, two-outcome market. Each row assumes that the chosen team wins an ordinary cash single bet. The $10 amount makes the arithmetic easy to follow; it is not a suggested stake.

Selection American price Stake Profit if it wins Total returned if it wins
Team A -150 $10.00 $6.67* $16.67*
Team B +130 $10.00 $13.00 $23.00

Displayed figures are rounded to cents. Actual operator rounding may vary.

A negative American price uses a reference ratio: -150 means $150 staked to earn $100 profit. The $100 is a unit for explaining the price, not a required wager. For the fictional $10 stake:

Profit = $10 × 100 ÷ 150 = $6.666…, or about $6.67.

If Team A wins, the original $10 comes back as well. That makes the total returned about $16.67. Calling $16.67 the profit would count your own stake as new money.

A positive price works the other way around: +130 means $130 profit on a $100 stake. Scale that ratio to the same fictional $10:

Profit = $10 × 130 ÷ 100 = $13.00.

Add the returned stake and the total is $23.00 if Team B wins. Here, $13 is profit and $23 is total return. These ratios follow DraftKings’ explanation of American odds.

If either ordinary cash single loses, the returned amount is $0 and the net change is -$10. Where the applicable rules provide a push, the stake comes back: $10 returned, $0 profit. Promotional bets, cash-out offers, fees and taxes are outside this example.

Two-way or three-way? Read the period

A two-way moneyline presents two selections. A three-way market offers the draw as a separate selection, alongside the two teams. A team-win selection and a draw selection are different outcomes. DraftKings’ moneyline explanation describes both formats.

Read the period named on the market and the applicable rules for ties and overtime; the word “moneyline” alone does not settle those details. Before reading the price, identify the period, check whether a draw is an available choice, and find the relevant settlement rules. If those terms are unclear, stop there rather than assuming that every tie returns the stake or that overtime always counts.

Before reading any price

  1. Name the selection and market. Who or what must win?
  2. Identify the period and draw handling. Which part of the game is covered, and is a draw a separate choice?
  3. Separate stake, profit and total return. Returned stake is not profit.
  4. Read the applicable rules. Stop if a term or settlement condition is unclear.

That sequence keeps a payout calculation from becoming an assumption about the result. A price can explain a potential return; it cannot promise a winner.

Common questions

Do I have to bet $100?

No. It is the reference unit used to explain American odds. The fictional $10 examples scale the ratio. Actual limits depend on the operator.

Does -150 mean the team must win by 150 points?

No. It is a price for the selected market, not a point spread.

Does the favorite always win?

No. The label and price do not guarantee a result.

Is every positive price an underdog?

No. A positive price can appear for the shortest-priced selection in a multi-outcome market. Compare the entire market; the sign is not a universal favorite test.

A moneyline bet names who wins the stated game, match, or period. Its price changes the potential profit, not the points a team must win by. Read the winner selection first, then the price beside it.

Here is a fictional two-team example: Team A at minus one hundred fifty, and Team B at plus one hundred thirty. We will use ten dollars to make the arithmetic concrete. These are separate alternatives, not advice to bet both, or a recommendation to stake ten dollars.

Minus one hundred fifty means a reference ratio of one hundred fifty dollars staked to earn one hundred dollars profit. You do not have to bet one hundred dollars. Scale the ratio: ten times one hundred, divided by one hundred fifty, is about six dollars and sixty-seven cents profit.

If Team A wins, add the returned ten-dollar stake. The total returned is about sixteen dollars and sixty-seven cents. That total includes your original money. Six dollars and sixty-seven cents is the profit. We are rounding to cents; actual operator rounding may vary.

At plus one hundred thirty, the calculation is ten times one hundred thirty, divided by one hundred. That gives thirteen dollars profit. Add the ten-dollar stake, and the total returned is twenty-three dollars if Team B wins. Again, twenty-three is the total, not the profit.

A losing ordinary cash single returns zero, leaving a ten-dollar loss. A push, where the applicable rules provide one, returns the stake with no profit. These calculations explain potential payouts. They do not predict which team will win.

Read the draw option and the period. A three-way market offers a draw as a separate selection. Picking a team to win is different from picking that draw. Check which game period the market covers, and read its rules for ties and overtime.

The sign is not a universal favorite test. A positive-priced selection can still have the shortest price in a market with more than two outcomes. Compare the whole market. A favorite can lose, and a price never guarantees the result.
