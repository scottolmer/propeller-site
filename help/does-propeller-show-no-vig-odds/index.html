What no-vig odds actually tell you about a player prop

Last updated: 2026-10-11

By Scott Olmer, Founder; designated author and editorial owner. Produced and reviewed through automated AI workflows. Editorial policy.

Direct answer: No-vig probability is an estimate derived from a complete set of market prices after adjusting for the excess in their implied probabilities. It is not the true chance of a result or Propeller’s Confidence Score. Start by checking that both prices describe the same player, event, stat, line, source and market snapshot.

Check the pair before calculating

A player-prop line and a price answer different questions. The line names the statistical threshold: 25.5 points, for example. A price such as -120 describes the quoted return for one side. Knowing the threshold and an Under direction does not tell you that price.

For this two-sided calculation, collect the Over and Under quotes for the same player, game, stat, half-point line, source and snapshot. Check that settlement terms match. An Over at one book and an Under from a different time do not establish the single matched market used here.

If either quote is missing, stop: “Cannot normalize this two-sided example.” Do not fill the gap with zero, 50%, a confidence score or an older quote. Save the missing field so you know what to obtain next. A precise-looking answer made from mismatched inputs still describes the wrong question.

A fictional example, with every input named

Example Player C has a 25.5-point line for Fictional Game A on Fictional Board B: Over -120 and Under +100. Both quotes belong to the same invented snapshot, September 5, 2026 at 10:00 America/Chicago. The player, event, source, prices and timestamp are all fictional teaching inputs.

Assume two exhaustive outcomes with matching settlement terms and no push, void or exchange commission. These assumptions keep this exercise to one complete two-sided market. They are not a statement about any platform’s actual rules.

Convert the quotes, then remove the excess

With negative American odds -A, A is the positive magnitude: the amount risked to win 100. Convert using A ÷ (A + 100). For -120, that is 120 ÷ 220 = 6/11, or 54.55%. With positive odds +B, B is the profit quoted for risking 100. Convert using 100 ÷ (B + 100). For +100, that is 100 ÷ 200 = 1/2, or 50.00%.

Add those raw implied probabilities. Their exact sum is 23/22, displayed as 104.55%. The excess above 100% is 4.55 percentage points, called the overround. It describes the price pair; it is not a prediction of your personal loss or return.

Our stated teaching method is proportional normalization: divide each raw probability by their sum. Over becomes (6/11) ÷ (23/22) = 12/23 = 52.17%. Under becomes (1/2) ÷ (23/22) = 11/23 = 47.83%.

What can the result tell you?

Within the fictional pair and chosen method, Over receives 52.17% of the normalized total and Under 47.83%. That is a reproducible summary of those prices. It does not prove that the player’s true chance is 52.17%, that a selection has an edge or that it will win.

Removing the excess does not validate the market’s underlying judgment or refresh an old snapshot. Proportional normalization states how we allocate that excess; naming the method keeps its assumption visible. Before using the number as research context, check whether its inputs still describe the event and line you are examining.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I calculate this with just one side?

No. You can convert one quote to its raw implied probability, but this normalization requires the complete matched pair. Missing one side means “Cannot normalize this two-sided example.”

Does 52.17% mean the true chance of winning?

No. It is the fictional Over price’s share after proportional normalization. Prices and a margin-removal method do not establish the true chance of an outcome.

Can I use a pick’em payout multiplier instead?

No. An entry-level payout multiplier is not a pair of side prices for this two-outcome market. Do not substitute it into the formulas.

Is no-vig probability the Confidence Score?

No. No-vig probability here comes from market prices. Propeller’s Confidence Score describes model support for a direction and is not a calibrated win probability.
